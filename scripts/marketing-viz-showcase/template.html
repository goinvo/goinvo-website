The problem

A few hours of marketing a week, and none of it said what to do on Monday

The studio needed outreach to lead: calls and emails to people who already know its work. Nobody on the team is a full-time marketer, cold calling is stressful, and the weekly budget for all of it is a handful of hours.

The records existed, spread across a contact list, a call log, web research on each organisation, a task board, a content calendar, a Slack channel and a date the money was certain until. Every number reached a person as a sentence, if it reached them at all.

The team asked for a marketer named Marqueta that runs the week: it plans the work against the hours people actually have, asks colleagues to take tasks in Slack, preps calls on request, and checks in on strategy and money.

The system

One set of records, two places people see it

Marqueta is a set of scheduled and on-request jobs over the studio’s own records. The Studio and Slack show the same numbers from the same code, so a figure on Monday’s message is the figure on This week.

Decision 1 of 3

Every chart says what to do

Each chart comes with one sentence that names the next move: start with the late follow-ups, fix the step where people drop out, re-check a runway nobody has confirmed in two months. The chart shows why.

The week at a glance

The first screen of the Studio, and where most Slack links land. Four questions in order: where the hours go, whether outreach is happening, who is owed a call, and how long the money lasts.

A bullet chart against the budget

The hours are split by what they are for, and the unspent time is left as track. “Can we take one more call?” is answered by the picture.

A week in progress is never “down”

On a Thursday the figure is set against last week’s total as a target to beat. Calling it a drop would be false three days early.

Status is an icon and a word

Overdue follow-ups are the dots left of today and the tile says “8 overdue”. Colour is never the only signal.

One line per task

The work list became a table with aligned columns. The bar in each time cell shows the week’s hours down the right edge, in the same colour as the task’s share of the meter above.

Outreach, read as shapes

Where people drop out, where the network is warm, and whether calling is a habit yet. Every chart has a table view, and every value in a tooltip is also reachable without hovering.

The funnel counts the furthest stage ever reached

A contact who met the team and has since gone quiet still counts as met. A logged call proves contact even when nobody moved the status. The weakest step with at least three people behind it is named, so a 0-of-1 step does not read as a finding.

Empty rows stay in the grid

A segment with plenty of names and nobody warm is the finding, so every segment keeps its row at zero.

The runway as a calendar

The financial posture used to be a label (“survival”) set once and never revisited. A label does not decay. The stored fact is now a date, the last day the studio is certain it can pay for, and the posture is computed from it. On a calendar, “4.5 months” becomes a plan: the day the strategy should tighten, and the signed work that bought time.

When the content calendar runs dry

A card per channel used to say “covered” or “coverage gap”. One grid of channels against weeks shows when each one runs out, which is the thing to act on.

Decision 2 of 3

Only what can be said on a call

A nervous caller repeats what the call sheet says. So a research claim reaches the call sheet only when its evidence can be inspected, never because a model agreed with it.

Two checks that must not be merged

First, the quoted words must literally appear on the page the claim cites: a fetch and a string match, no model. Then the claim may not assert any number, name or date the quote does not contain. A model asked to confirm a quote it just produced will confirm it.

Unverified is shown, and labelled

Claims that fail are not deleted. They appear as background, marked “not verified, do not repeat as fact”, and call prep never uses them as a line to say.

A failed fetch is not a failed claim

Paywalls and bot walls are common. When too few cited pages could be read, the claim is “unchecked”, never “unsupported”; blaming the research for the network would teach the team to ignore the check.

Private by routing, not by luck

Contacts, research and the runway live in a private dataset. One routing rule decides where every record type goes, and a test fails if a new type is added without a route.

Decision 3 of 3

A marketer nobody mutes

A bot in a team channel has one chance. Marqueta asks rather than assigns, stays quiet when there is nothing to say, and fits the Monday plan to a phone held before the first coffee.

Asks, not assignments

An unowned task goes to one named, available person, never the same person twice. “Not me” records a pass without explanation; after two passes the task is offered for dropping.

Quiet where people work

In the team’s own channels she catches proposals silently, for review in one batch. “Thanks!” gets no reply at all. A typo gets a suggested command, which is never run on a guess.

A stat grid in a medium with no charts

Slack cannot draw a chart, so last week becomes four fields laid out two by two. Figures are bold and lead; the words after them say what was counted.

Held to a phone screen

A test renders the message at forty characters a line. The first “I’ll take it” has to appear inside thirty lines and the whole plan inside eighty, so the numbers grid had to earn its lines.

What happened

What real data changed

Each of these came from running the system against the studio’s real messages and records, not from a mock-up. Most of them changed a design decision.

22 → 7

The idea filter, replayed on 189 real messages

It would have posted 22 guesses in the team’s channel, and about 7 were worth keeping. A bot that is wrong two times in three gets muted, so in human channels it now captures silently.

0 of 20

Research claims verified under the first prompt

Not invented, but over-specified: true in substance while asserting more than the page said. The prompt now forbids any number, name or date that is not in the quote.

~100 → 30

Phone lines before the first “I’ll take it”

Slack draws attachments below every block, so the first ask sat far down the message. Rebuilt as blocks in the order people act, with a test that fails if the first ask drops below line 30.

60 → 16

Lines before “Log it…” on a call outline

The button to record how a call went moved up under the ten-second cheat sheet, where a caller’s thumb already is.

2

Bugs only real text could find

“bul-lets on them” matched the proposal marker “lets”, and “mo-ooo-ore” matched the away marker “ooo”. Markers now match on word boundaries.

0

Scheduled jobs that had ever run on their own

Everything had been triggered by hand. A Monday run now records what it did, and a watchdog outside the host emails if it goes quiet.

Colour

A palette that was computed, not picked

The studio’s teal and orange are too muted to tell chart series apart. Every chart colour was generated from those two hues and tested for colour-blind readers in both themes before it shipped.

Rules

Rules the system follows

A failed read is “unknown”, never zero

“Could not read the contacts” and “nobody is owed a call” look identical as a number. Every figure that can fail to load says so instead.

Progress is a move, not a touch

A voicemail left for a contact already in a meeting is not a new meeting. The pulse counts changes of state, walked in time order.

Colour follows the entity

Outreach is orange in the meter, the table and the legend, every week. A filter never repaints the survivors.

The work that did not fit is shown

A plan that hides what it dropped is a filter. Deferred work is listed with the reason it waits.

Silence is a signal

An empty Thursday still posts one line, because a missing message looks exactly like a job that died.

A person decides

Marqueta files proposals and asks questions. Keeping an idea, taking a task and confirming the runway are always a person’s press.
